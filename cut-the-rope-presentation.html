So, what's the game?

Imagine a ball, dangling from ropes. Your only tool? A pair of scissors.

Cut the right ropes, at the right time, and gravity does the rest. Land the ball in the glowing green target — and hold it there for two full seconds.

Sounds easy? It's not. Spikes, momentum, and your own impatience stand in the way.

Press A, B, C… to cut each rope. Press R when things go wrong (they will).

More puzzle than action

Why it's a puzzle game

  • Each level has one (or very few) valid solutions
  • Success comes from reasoning, not reflexes
  • Clear binary outcomes — you win or you don't
  • No randomness; the physics are deterministic
It's a puzzle game with progression elements. Think Angry Birds, not Call of Duty.

Why does cutting ropes feel good?

The "aha!" moment

You stare at a level, confused. Then you see it — cut B first, then A — and the ball arcs perfectly into the target. That dopamine hit? That's the whole game.

Instant feedback

Cut a rope and the ball moves immediately. No menus, no delays. You see the consequence of your action in real time.

Visible progress

Six levels, each harder than the last. You can feel yourself getting better — reading the physics, predicting the swing.

Emergent complexity

Two simple rules — gravity and rope tension — create wildly different challenges depending on anchor placement.

Fun isn't in the destination. It's in the figuring out.

How designers build experiences — and how players receive them.

Mechanics — the raw ingredients

These are the rules we coded. They don't know they're fun — that's the player's job.

Rope Cutting

  • Each rope mapped to a keyboard letter
  • Instant cut with particle burst
  • Rope disappears, ball is released

Physics Engine

  • Gravity: −900 px/s²
  • Pymunk handles collisions
  • 4 sub-steps per frame for accuracy

Win Condition

  • Ball inside target zone
  • Speed drops below 80 px/s
  • Stays settled for 2 full seconds

Fail States

  • Touch any spike → instant death
  • Fall off screen → level lost
  • Press R to instantly retry

Dynamics — what actually happens

Mechanics on paper are boring. Dynamics are what happen when a player gets their hands on them.

Emergent motion

Cut one rope and the ball swings on the other. Cut the second mid-swing and the ball flies across the screen. Chain reactions emerge from two simple cuts.

Timing becomes everything

The same cut at different moments leads to completely different outcomes. Players learn to "feel" the right moment — that's a dynamic, not a rule.

The retry loop

Fail, reset, adjust, retry. Each attempt teaches something. The quick reset means frustration never builds — you're always one keypress from another try.

Aesthetics — what players feel

Challenge

The core emotion. Every level asks: "Can you figure this out?" And the answer is always uncertain until the ball finally settles.

Discovery

Finding the right solution feels like uncovering a secret. "Oh — I need to cut C first, then wait for the swing."

Sensation

The bounce, the swing, the satisfying "thunk" when the ball lands. Physics feedback makes every action feel tactile.

The gap between what we designed and what players feel is where game design actually happens.

How players learn without a tutorial

Schema theory in action

Players build mental models through play. Nobody reads a manual — they experiment and pattern-match.

  • Cut a rope → ball swings on the other one
  • Ball has momentum → it carries forward after release
  • Timing matters → same cut, different outcomes

Our design trick

We teach patterns in early levels, then break those patterns in later ones.

Level 1 says: "Cut the rope, ball falls down." Level 5 says: "Actually, you need to swing it sideways first."

That subversion is where the puzzle lives.

Keeping players in the zone

Flow theory says:

Too easy → boredom. Too hard → frustration. The sweet spot in between? That's flow.

  • Levels 1–2: Gentle intro. Learn the basics without pressure.
  • Levels 3–4: The sweet spot. Challenge matches growing skill.
  • Levels 5–6: Demanding. Requires mastery of timing and momentum.
The instant reset (R key) is our secret weapon — it prevents frustration from kicking players out of flow.

What we learned building this

01

A strong core mechanic carries everything

Rope-cutting is intuitive, satisfying, and generates complexity on its own. We didn't need a hundred features — we needed one good one.

02

Feedback needs to be instant

The 2-second settle timer creates real tension. But the instant reset removes frustration. That balance is the whole trick.

03

Let players fail cheaply

Quick reset keeps people experimenting. The moment failure feels expensive, players stop taking risks.

04

Teach through play, not text

No tutorials. Level 1 teaches gravity. Level 2 teaches swinging. Level 3 introduces danger. The levels are the tutorial.

Room to grow

Social features, level sharing, story elements — there's plenty of space to expand. But the core loop works.
